## Overview

A demon is, broadly, a supernatural being whose function in a religious or folkloric system is to harm, tempt, or oppose humans, the divine order, or both. The English word descends from Greek δαίμων (daimōn), originally a morally neutral term for an intermediate divine being between gods and humans, used by Hesiod, Plato, and the Pre-Socratics. Hellenistic and early Christian writers narrowed the term to malevolent spirits, conflating it with Hebrew shedim and se'irim and with the fallen-angel traditions of Second-Temple Jewish apocalyptic literature (1 Enoch, Jubilees). Christian theologians (Augustine of Hippo, Aquinas) systematised demons as fallen angels under Lucifer/Satan; medieval and Renaissance demonology produced extensive hierarchies (Pseudomonarchia Daemonum, 1577; Lesser Key of Solomon, 17th century) and lists of named demons (Asmodeus, Belial, Beelzebub, Astaroth, Belphegor). Islamic tradition distinguishes shayāṭīn (devils, with Iblīs as their head) from jinn (a separate created order with free will, capable of being good or evil). Zoroastrianism inverts the older Indo-Iranian deva/asura distinction so that the daēvas become demons under Angra Mainyu. Hindu cosmology recognises asuras and rākshasas, and Buddhism the māras and naraka-beings, but in both traditions such beings are subject to karma and rebirth rather than being eternally damned. Folkloric demons appear in Mesopotamian texts (utukku, Lamaštu, Pazuzu), in Japanese tradition (oni, yōkai), and across many other cultures.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Greek δαίμων (daimōn), of disputed root: possibly from δαίεσθαι 'to divide, distribute', i.e. 'distributor of fates'. The Latin daemon was theologically narrowed to evil spirits in patristic and medieval Christian usage.
- **Meaning**: In classical Greek, an intermediate divine being or guardian spirit; in later Christian theology, an evil supernatural being or fallen angel.
- **First use**: Greek daimōn attested in Homer (8th century BCE) and Hesiod; the narrow 'evil-spirit' sense crystallises in Greek-Jewish and Christian texts of the Hellenistic and early imperial periods.

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Real spiritual beings** _(Major Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Islamic theologians)_

In Christian, Islamic, Jewish, and many other religious systems, demons are understood as real spiritual beings, ontologically distinct from humans and angels, who oppose the divine order.

**Fallen-angel doctrine** _(Augustine, Aquinas, al-Ghazālī)_

Christian and post-Quranic Islamic traditions hold that demons are angels who rebelled against God under a leader (Satan / Iblīs).

### Skeptical

**Cultural and psychological framing** _(I. M. Lewis, Erika Bourguignon, Pieter F. Craffert)_

Demonological phenomena (possession, attack, oppression) are interpreted by anthropologists and clinicians as a religiously-coded language for psychological, neurological, and social distress.

**Comparative-religion perspective** _(Wendy Doniger, Jeffrey Burton Russell)_

'Demons' are a comparative category that obscures the very different conceptions of harmful spiritual beings across cultures (asuras, jinn, oni, etc.); careful religious-studies work prefers tradition-specific terminology.
